With SIS and LWE at hand, we can build lattice-based analogues of the discrete-logarithm (DL) protocols from the previous chapters (specifically, \Cref{section:sigma}).
Surprisingly, the considered techniques greatly resemble DL-based protocols, to the point that they follow exactly the same steps with the only exception of the so-called \emph{rejection sampling}.
That said, the key idea of this section is as follows:

\begin{bookquote}
    One can translate the majority of existing DL-based protocols to lattice-based assumptions by employing the rejecting sampling technique.
\end{bookquote}

Of course, there is a caveat: resulting schemes are commonly largely inefficient.
However, by exploiting the algebraic structure of cyclotomic rings (\Cref{subsec:cyclotomic-rings}), one can significantly optimize these protocols.

\subsection{Lattice-Based Identification and Rejection Sampling}\label{section:lyubashevsky}

We begin with arguably the most basic construction: Schnorr identification protocol.
By translating DL-based approach to lattices, one obtains the Lyubashevsky~\cite{EC:Lyubashevsky12} identification protocol. 
After applying the Fiat-Shamir transform it becomes a signature scheme.

\begin{remark}
    Interestingly, one almost immediately obtains the NIST-standardized Dilithium signature scheme~\cite{TCHES:DKLLSS18} by changing the underlying Gaussian distribution to the uniform one.
\end{remark}

In \Cref{section:als20,section:lnp22} we then extend the same protocol from proving knowledge of a secret key to proving linear and quadratic relations among committed values.

For simplicity, in this section we work over $\mathbb{Z}_q$; the ring and module versions are obtained by replacing $\mathbb{Z}_q$ with $\mathcal{R}_q$.
Besides the set of short elements $S_{\eta}$, we use the \textbf{challenge set}
\begin{equation*}
    B_{\omega} := \{\mathbf{c} \in \{-1,0,1\}^k: \mathbf{c} \; \text{has exactly} \; \omega \; \text{non-zero entries}\}.
\end{equation*}
where the weight $\omega$ is chosen so that $\#B_{\omega} \geq 2^{\lambda}$.

\begin{exercise}\label{exercise:challenge-set}
    Verify the following two facts:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\#B_{\omega} = 2^{\omega}\binom{k}{\omega}$;
        \item $\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}\|_{\infty} \leq \eta\omega$ for every $\mathbf{S} \in S_{\eta}^{m \times k}$ and $\mathbf{c} \in B_{\omega}$.
    \end{enumerate}
    These two facts will be used when describing the Lyubashevsky scheme.
\end{exercise}

Finally, in the subsequent interactive protocols, we use \colorbox{oc-blue-1}{$\mathcal{P}(\mathbbm{x}, \mathbbm{w})$} to denote prover's actions while \colorbox{oc-blue-1}{$\mathcal{V}(\mathbbm{x})$} to denote verifier's actions.

\subsubsection{From Schnorr to Lattices}

Recall the Schnorr identification protocol over a group $\mathbb{G} = \langle g \rangle$ of prime order $q$.
The prover holds a secret key $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_q$, the verifier holds the public key $h = g^{\alpha}$, and the prover wants to convince the verifier that it knows $\alpha$.
The protocol is specified in \Cref{fig:schnorr-id}.
For more details, make sure to check \Cref{subsec:schnorr-identification}.

Two properties make this protocol secure.
First, the map $\alpha \mapsto g^{\alpha}$ is one-way, and from two accepting transcripts $(w,c,z)$ and $(w,c',z')$ with $c \neq c'$ one extracts $\alpha = (z-z')/(c-c')$ (special soundness).
Second, $z = r + \alpha c$ is uniform in $\mathbb{Z}_q$ for every fixed $\alpha$, so the transcript reveals nothing about $\alpha$ (zero-knowledge).
We now try to preserve both properties in the lattice setting.

\paragraph{Attempt \#1: replace exponentiation with a matrix}
A good candidate for the lattice analogue of $\alpha \mapsto g^{\alpha}$ is the linear map $\mathbf{S} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}$ for a public random matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times m}$.
The secret key becomes $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{m \times k}$, the public key becomes $\mathbf{T} := \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}$, and the challenge becomes a vector $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^k$.
Otherwise, the protocol in \Cref{fig:attempt-1} is a line-by-line copy of \Cref{fig:schnorr-id}; we highlight the changes with \colorbox{oc-orange-1}{orange boxes}.

The protocol is correct, since $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{T}\mathbf{c}$.
It is also zero-knowledge, since $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$ is uniform in $\mathbb{Z}_q^m$ for every fixed $\mathbf{S}$, exactly as in Schnorr.
However, it is completely unsound: the verification equation is linear, so anyone can pass it without knowing $\mathbf{S}$.
For example, since $m > n$, a cheating prover can find \emph{some} matrix $\mathbf{S}'$ with $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}' = \mathbf{T}$ by Gaussian elimination and then run the honest prover with $\mathbf{S}'$.
The underlying reason is that the map $\mathbf{S} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}$ is one-way only on \emph{short} inputs, by (I)SIS (see corresponding paragraph in \Cref{section:sis}).

\paragraph{Attempt \#2: make everything short}
We therefore make the secret short, $\mathbf{S} \leftarrowS S_{\eta}^{m \times k}$, and require the verifier to check that $\mathbf{z}$ is short, so that a long $\mathbf{S}'$ found by Gaussian elimination is useless.
For an honest $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$ to pass this check, both terms must be short:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the challenge is sampled from $B_{\omega}$, so that $\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}\|_{\infty} \leq \eta\omega$ by \Cref{exercise:challenge-set};
    \item the masking vector $\mathbf{r}$ is sampled from some \emph{short} distribution $\chi$ over $\mathbb{Z}^m$;
    \item the response $\mathbf{z}$ is computed over $\mathbb{Z}$, without reduction modulo $q$.
\end{itemize}
The resulting protocol is specified in \Cref{fig:attempt-2}.

This protocol is now sound, and the argument is the same as for Schnorr.
Given two accepting transcripts $(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{z})$ and $(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{c}',\mathbf{z}')$ with $\mathbf{c} \neq \mathbf{c}'$, subtracting the verification equations gives $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}') = \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}')$, and substituting $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}$ yields
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{A}\bigl(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}' - \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}')\bigr) = \mathbf{0} \pmod q.
\end{equation*}
The vector in parentheses is short (its norm is at most $2\beta + 2\eta\omega\sqrt{m}$), so unless it is zero, it is a solution to SIS.
This is the same argument as for the binding property of the Ajtai commitment in \Cref{exercise:ajtai-collision}.

Unfortunately, the protocol is no longer zero-knowledge: \emph{the response $\mathbf{z}$ leaks the secret key}.
A short $\mathbf{r}$ cannot hide the shift $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$ completely.
For example, if $\chi$ is centered at zero, then $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}] = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$, and since $\mathbf{c}$ is public, a verifier who collects enough transcripts recovers $\mathbf{S}$ by linear regression.
\Cref{fig:leakage} illustrates the problem: the distribution of $\mathbf{z}$ is the distribution $\chi$ shifted by the secret-dependent vector $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$.
In Schnorr this problem never arises, because $r$ is uniform over the whole group $\mathbb{Z}_q$, and a uniform distribution shifted by $\alpha c$ is again uniform.

\paragraph{Attempt \#3: rejection sampling}
The fix is to let the prover \emph{abort} whenever the candidate response $\mathbf{z}$ reveals too much about $\mathbf{S}$, and restart the protocol with a fresh mask.
The abort probability is chosen so that every response that the prover \emph{does} send follows a fixed distribution independent of $\mathbf{S}$.
Since the aborts become restarts of the signing procedure after the Fiat-Shamir transform, this approach is called \textbf{Fiat-Shamir with aborts}.
The tool that computes the right abort probability is the following classical lemma, going back to von Neumann.

\begin{lemma}[Rejection Sampling]\label{lemma:rejection-sampling}
    Let $f$ and $g$ be probability distributions over a set $V$ such that $f(z) \leq M g(z)$ for all $z \in V$ and some constant $M \geq 1$.
    Consider the following procedure:
    \begin{algoen}
        \item sample $z \leftarrowS g$;
        \item with probability $\frac{f(z)}{Mg(z)}$, \keyword{output} $z$; otherwise, \keyword{goto} step 1.
    \end{algoen}
    The output of this procedure is distributed exactly according to $f$, and the expected number of iterations is $M$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    In a single iteration, the procedure samples a particular $z$ and accepts it with probability $g(z) \cdot \frac{f(z)}{Mg(z)} = \frac{f(z)}{M}$.
    Summing over all $z$, one iteration succeeds with probability $\frac{1}{M}$, so the expected number of iterations is $M$.
    Conditioned on success, $z$ is output with probability $\frac{f(z)/M}{1/M} = f(z)$.
\end{proof}

In our setting, $g$ is the \emph{real} distribution of the candidate $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$ (which depends on $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$), and $f$ is a \emph{target} distribution that does not depend on $\mathbf{S}$.
\Cref{fig:rejection-sampling} gives the geometric picture behind the lemma.
Sampling $z \leftarrowS g$ and then $u \leftarrowS [0, Mg(z)]$ produces a uniformly random point $(z,u)$ under the graph of $Mg$.
The point is accepted if and only if it also lies under the graph of $f$, and accepted points are uniform under the graph of $f$, so their first coordinates follow $f$.
The rejected points form the hatched region, and the ratio between the areas under $Mg$ and under $f$ is exactly $M$.
Hence, the closer $g$ resembles $f$, the smaller $M$ can be chosen, and the fewer restarts the prover needs.

To apply \Cref{lemma:rejection-sampling}, we need $f \leq Mg$ for a small constant $M$ and \emph{every} possible shift $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$, and this condition determines how to choose the distribution $\chi$ of the mask.
The standard choice, used by Lyubashevsky's scheme and by most lattice-based zero-knowledge proofs, is the discrete Gaussian distribution.

\subsubsection{Discrete Gaussians and Gaussian Rejection Sampling}

\begin{definition}[Discrete Gaussian]\label{def:discrete-gaussian}
    For $\sigma > 0$ and $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{Z}^m$, let 
    \begin{equation*}
        \rho_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}(\mathbf{x}) := \exp\left(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}\|_2^2/2\sigma^2\right) \; \text{for $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m$}.   
    \end{equation*}
    The \textbf{discrete Gaussian distribution} over $\mathbb{Z}^m$ with center $\mathbf{v}$ and width $\sigma$ assigns to each $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{Z}^m$ the probability
    \begin{equation*}
        \dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{\rho_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{Z}^m}\rho_{\sigma}(\mathbf{y})}.
    \end{equation*}
    We omit $\mathbf{v}$ when $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ and write $\rho_{\sigma} := \rho_{\mathbf{0},\sigma}$ and $\dgaussian^m_{\sigma} := \dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{0},\sigma}$.
\end{definition}

The normalization is the same for every integer center $\mathbf{v}$, since shifting by $\mathbf{v}$ permutes $\mathbb{Z}^m$.
Informally, $\dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}$ is the usual bell-shaped Gaussian centered at $\mathbf{v}$, restricted to integer points, as shown in \Cref{fig:discrete-gaussian}\footnote{Some works, including many theoretical lattice papers, write the Gaussian as $\exp(-\pi\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2/s^2)$ (the latter is more pleasant to work with when applying Fourier-analytic machinery). The two conventions are related by $s = \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma$.}.
More generally, one can define the discrete Gaussian over any lattice $\Lambda$ by restricting $\rho_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}$ to the points of $\Lambda$; \Cref{fig:discrete-gaussian} uses a non-orthogonal lattice to emphasize this.

We need two properties of the discrete Gaussian distribution.
The first one states that samples from the discrete Gaussian are short with overwhelming probability.

\begin{lemma}[Lemma 4.4~\cite{EC:Lyubashevsky12}]\label{lemma:gaussian-norm}
    For any $\tau > 1$, one has
    \begin{equation*}
        \Pr_{\mathbf{z} \leftarrowS \dgaussian^m_{\sigma}}\left[\|\mathbf{z}\|_2 > \tau\sigma\sqrt{m}\right] < \tau^m e^{m(1-\tau^2)/2}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

The right-hand side is negligible in $m$ even for $\tau = 2$ as $2^me^{-3m/2}< 2^{-m}$.
Therefore, the verifier can safely reject any response with $\|\mathbf{z}\|_2 > 2\sigma\sqrt{m}$ (although in practice one uses a threshold $\tau\sigma\sqrt{m}$ for some $\tau$ between $1.0$ and $2.0$).

The second property adapts \Cref{lemma:rejection-sampling} to the discrete Gaussian.
It states that rejection sampling hides a short shift $\mathbf{v}$, and specifies the value of $M$.

\begin{lemma}[Lemma 4.5~\cite{EC:Lyubashevsky12}]\label{lemma:gaussian-rejection}
    Let $V \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^m$ be a set of vectors with $\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \leq T$ for all $\mathbf{v} \in V$, and let $h$ be a probability distribution over $V$.
    Let $\sigma = \alpha T$ for some $\alpha > 0$, and set $M := \exp\left(\frac{12}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{2\alpha^2}\right)$.
    Then, the statistical distance between the following two algorithms $\mathcal{A}_0$ and $\mathcal{A}_1$ is at most $2^{-100}/M$:

    \medskip
    \noindent
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.46\linewidth} p{0.46\linewidth}}
    \underline{\textbf{Algorithm $\mathcal{A}_0$}} & \underline{\textbf{Algorithm $\mathcal{A}_1$}} \\[-1ex]
    \begin{algoen}
        \item $\mathbf{v} \leftarrowS h$;
        \item $\mathbf{z} \leftarrowS \dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}$;
        \item $u \leftarrowS (0,1)$;
        \item \keyword{if} $u > \frac{\dgaussian^m_{\sigma}(\mathbf{z})}{M\dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}(\mathbf{z})}$: \keyword{goto} \textcolor{gray}{step 1};
        \item \keyword{return} $(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{v})$.
    \end{algoen}
    &
    \begin{algoen}
        \item $\mathbf{v} \leftarrowS h$;
        \item $\mathbf{z} \leftarrowS \dgaussian^m_{\sigma}$;
        \item $u \leftarrowS (0,1)$;
        \item \keyword{if} $u > 1/M$: \keyword{goto} \textcolor{gray}{step 1};
        \item \keyword{return} $(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{v})$.
    \end{algoen}
    \end{tabular}

    Moreover, the probability that $\mathcal{A}_0$ outputs something is at least $(1-2^{-100})/M$.
\end{lemma}

In $\mathcal{A}_1$, the output $\mathbf{z}$ is independent of $\mathbf{v}$.
Hence, the lemma says that the responses accepted by the real prover ($\mathcal{A}_0$) reveal nothing about the shift, up to statistical distance $2^{-100}/M$.

Let us briefly explain why the lemma holds.
The ratio between the target and the real distribution is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\dgaussian^m_{\sigma}(\mathbf{z})}{\dgaussian^m_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}(\mathbf{z})} = \exp\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_2^2 - 2\langle \mathbf{z},\mathbf{v} \rangle}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\end{equation*}
which depends on $\mathbf{z}$ only through the projection $\langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$.
In other words, the two distributions coincide along $\mathbf{v}^{\perp}$, and the problem reduces to the one-dimensional Gaussian since $\langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{z} \rangle$ behaves like a one-dimensional Gaussian.
The ratio is not bounded in the far left tail, so the condition $f \leq Mg$ of \Cref{lemma:rejection-sampling} holds only with overwhelming probability.
However, $\langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{v} \rangle$ behaves like a one-dimensional Gaussian of width $\sigma\|\mathbf{v}\|_2$, so $|\langle \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq 12\sigma\|\mathbf{v}\|_2$ except with probability $2^{-100}$ (this is just a rough technical result; see \cite[Lemma 4.3]{EC:Lyubashevsky12} for more details), and on this event the ratio is at most $M$.

% --- figure fig:gaussian-rejection unchanged ---

In the identification protocol, the shift is $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}$ with $\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}\|_2 \leq \sqrt{m}\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}\|_{\infty} \leq \eta\omega\sqrt{m} =: T$ by \Cref{exercise:challenge-set}.
Taking $\sigma = 12T$, \Cref{lemma:gaussian-rejection} gives $M = e^{1+1/288} \approx \exp(1) \approx 2.72$, so the prover restarts fewer than three times on average.
We package the acceptance test of $\mathcal{A}_0$ into the algorithm $\mathsf{Rej}$ of \Cref{fig:rej}, which we reuse in all subsequent protocols.

Let us check the three properties of a $\Sigma$-protocol.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Completeness.} The equation $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{T}\mathbf{c}$ holds as before. The accepted $\mathbf{z}$ is distributed (up to negligible statistical distance) as $\dgaussian^m_{\sigma}$, so by \Cref{lemma:gaussian-norm} with $\tau = 2$ the norm check fails with a negligible probability. The prover needs $M \approx 2.72$ attempts on average.
    \item \textbf{Zero-knowledge.} Conditioned on no abort, $\mathbf{z}$ is distributed as $\dgaussian^m_{\sigma}$ independently of $\mathbf{S}$ thanks to \Cref{lemma:gaussian-rejection}. Hence, a simulator samples $\mathbf{c} \leftarrowS B_{\omega}$ and $\mathbf{z} \leftarrowS \dgaussian^m_{\sigma}$ directly and sets $\mathbf{w} := \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{T}\mathbf{c}$, which is the only value consistent with the verification equation.
    \item \textbf{Soundness.} One applied the extraction argument of Attempt \#2.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Relaxed extraction]\label{remark:relaxed-extraction}
    Notice what the extractor actually obtains: not the secret $\mathbf{S}$ (as in the case of Schnorr), but the pair $(\bar{\mathbf{z}}, \bar{\mathbf{c}}) := (\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}')$ with $\mathbf{A}\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{T}\bar{\mathbf{c}}$.
    The extracted vector $\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ is about $\sigma/\eta$ times longer than the honest secret, and it satisfies the relation only after multiplying $\mathbf{T}$ by the difference of challenges $\bar{\mathbf{c}}$.
    This gap between what the honest prover knows and what the extractor can guarantee is inherent to lattice-based proofs of knowledge (it is often called \emph{soundness slack}), and it will be important in \Cref{section:als20,section:lnp22}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Performance}
For about $100$ bits of security, Lyubashevsky~\cite{EC:Lyubashevsky12} proposes, among others, the parameters $n = 512$, $q = 2^{27}$, $m = 8786$, $k = 80$, $\eta = 1$, and $\omega = 28$, which give $\sigma \approx 31495$.
The resulting signatures take about $19.9$~KB and the keys about $128$~KB each, which is far from practical.
Most of the size comes from the unstructured matrix and the large dimension $m$.
Moving to Module-SIS and Module-LWE over $\mathcal{R}_q$ (\Cref{section:structured-lattices}), switching to uniform masks, and compressing the public key and the signature yield ML-DSA (Dilithium)~\cite{TCHES:DKLLSS18}, standardized in FIPS~204, with signatures of about $2.4$~KB and public keys of about $1.3$~KB at its lowest security level.

\subsection{Proving Products of Committed Values}\label{section:als20}

Similarly to Schnorr protocol, the Lyubashevsky protocol proves knowledge of a short preimage of a public value.
As we have already observed throughout the book, zero-knowledge applications need much more: the prover commits to secret values and then proves that they satisfy some fairly complicated relations.
For example, a confidential transaction commits to amounts and proves that each amount is in range, which boils down to proving that its bits $b_i$ satisfy \emph{quadratic relations} $b_i(1-b_i) = 0$.

In this section, we present the lattice analogue due to Attema, Lyubashevsky, and Seiler~\cite{C:AttLyuSei20}, which we simply refer to as \emph{ALS20}, which proves multiplicative relations $\mathbf{a} \odot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{c}$ (for $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^d$) between committed values.
The construction has two ingredients: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a commitment scheme with an efficient opening proof;
    \item linearizing the quadratic constraint using masked openings.
\end{enumerate}

From now on, we work over the ring $\mathcal{R}_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[T]/(T^d+1)$ from \Cref{section:structured-lattices}, where $d$ is a power of two; bold lowercase letters such as $\mathbf{r}$ denote vectors over $\mathcal{R}_q$, and sans-serif letters such as $\mathsf{T}$ denote commitments.

\subsubsection{The BDLOP Commitment Scheme}

The Ajtai commitment from \Cref{section:sis} requires the \emph{message} to be short, since binding reduces to SIS (\Cref{exercise:ajtai-collision}).
This is inconvenient here: we want to commit to arbitrary elements of $\mathcal{R}_q$, for example to elements of $\mathbb{Z}_q$ packed into a polynomial.
The BDLOP commitment scheme~\cite{SCN:BDLOP18} removes this restriction by requiring only the \emph{randomness} to be short.

\begin{definition}[BDLOP Commitment]\label{def:bdlop}
    Define the \textbf{BDLOP commitment scheme} over the message space $\mathcal{R}_q^n$ as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda}) \to \mathsf{pp}$: sample a matrix $\mathbf{B}_0 \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^{\mu \times (\lambda+\mu+n)}$ and vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^{\lambda+\mu+n}$; output $\mathsf{pp} \gets (\mathbf{B}_0, \mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_n)$.
        \item $\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{pp}, \mathbf{m} \in \mathcal{R}_q^n) \to (\mathsf{T}, \mathbf{r})$: given a short distribution $\chi$, choose a short random vector $\mathbf{r} \leftarrowS \chi^{\lambda+\mu+n}$ and compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbf{t}_0 \gets \mathbf{B}_0\mathbf{r}, \quad t_i \gets \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{r} \rangle + m_i \; \text{for each} \; i \in [n].
        \end{equation*}
        Let $\mathsf{T} \gets (\mathbf{t}_0, t_1, \dots, t_n)$ and output $(\mathsf{T}, \mathbf{r})$.
    \end{itemize}
    Here, $\mu$ and $\lambda$ are the ranks of the underlying MSIS and MLWE instances, respectively.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Some properties} The two parts (namely, computing $\mathbf{t}_0$ and then $t_i$'s for $i>0$) of the commitment play different roles.
The \emph{binding part} $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{B}_0\mathbf{r}$ is an Ajtai-style commitment to the randomness: two different short openings $\mathbf{r} \neq \mathbf{r}'$ of $\mathbf{t}_0$ give the short solution $\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'$ to MSIS for $\mathbf{B}_0$.
Once $\mathbf{r}$ is fixed, the \emph{message part} $t_i = \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{r} \rangle + m_i$ determines $m_i$ uniquely. 
This essentially proves the \emph{binding property} of a BDLOP commitment scheme.

\emph{Hiding} follows from MLWE: the vector $(\mathbf{B}_0\mathbf{r}, \langle \mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{r}\rangle, \dots, \langle \mathbf{b}_n,\mathbf{r}\rangle)$ is indistinguishable from uniform, so it acts like a one-time pad on $\mathbf{m}$.
Notice that each additional message costs just one additional element $t_i \in \mathcal{R}_q$, and that the scheme is additively homomorphic: for public $\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \mathcal{R}_q$, the value $\alpha_1 t_1 + \alpha_2 t_2$ is a commitment to $\alpha_1 m_1 + \alpha_2 m_2$ with the vector $\alpha_1\mathbf{b}_1 + \alpha_2\mathbf{b}_2$.

\subsubsection{The Opening Proof}

To prove knowledge of an opening of $\mathsf{T}$, it suffices to prove knowledge of a short $\mathbf{r}$ with $\mathbf{B}_0\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{t}_0$, and this is exactly the Lyubashevsky protocol over $\mathcal{R}_q$ with a single secret vector (which we have just considered in \Cref{section:lyubashevsky}!)
The challenges now come from a set $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{R}_q$ of polynomials with small coefficients (for example, with coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$), and the rejection sampling is performed on the offset $c\mathbf{r}$.

\paragraph{Notes on security} As in \Cref{remark:relaxed-extraction}, two accepting transcripts $(\mathbf{w}, c, \mathbf{z})$ and $(\mathbf{w}, c', \mathbf{z}')$ give $\mathbf{B}_0\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \bar{c}\mathbf{t}_0$ for $\bar{\mathbf{z}} := \mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}'$ and $\bar{c} := c-c'$.
If $\bar{c}$ is invertible in $\mathcal{R}_q$, the extractor can define the randomness $\mathbf{r}^{\ast} := \bar{c}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ and the messages $m_i^{\ast} := t_i - \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{r}^{\ast} \rangle$.
The vector $\mathbf{r}^{\ast}$ is not necessarily short, since the inverse of a short polynomial is typically long (see Exercise below), but $\bar{c}\mathbf{r}^{\ast} = \bar{\mathbf{z}}$ is short, and one can show that this \emph{relaxed opening} is still binding under MSIS.

\begin{exercise}
    Let $f := 1-T$ be a short element in $\mathcal{R}_q$ (note that $\|f\|_{\infty}=1$). 
    Compute $\|f^{-1}\|_{\infty}$.
    Deduce that the inverse of a short element may be large.

    \commentgray{Answer: $\|f^{-1}\|_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2}(q-1) \approx \frac{1}{2}q$.}
\end{exercise}

ALS20 make one more crucial observation, which is the key to the product proof.
Once the prover has sent $\mathbf{w}$, it is committed not only to $\mathbf{r}^{\ast}$ but also to the mask $\mathbf{y}^{\ast} := \mathbf{z} - c\mathbf{r}^{\ast}$: unless the prover can solve MSIS, every accepting response to every challenge has the form $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y}^{\ast} + c\mathbf{r}^{\ast}$ with the \emph{same} $\mathbf{y}^{\ast}$.
Consequently, the verifier can compute from public data the values
\begin{equation*}
    f_i := \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{z} \rangle - ct_i = a_i - cm_i, \quad \text{where} \; a_i := \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{y} \rangle.
\end{equation*}

\begin{exercise}
    Verify that indeed $\langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{z} \rangle - ct_i = \langle \mathbf{b}_i, \mathbf{y} \rangle - cm_i$ for every $i \in [n]$.
\end{exercise}

Each $f_i$ is a \emph{masked opening} of the message $m_i$: an affine function of the challenge $c$ whose leading coefficient is the message itself, masked by the value $a_i$ that the prover fixed before seeing $c$.

\begin{remark}
    In previous product proofs, the prover had to commit to separate masks $a_i$ and send the masked openings together with a proof of their well-formedness; here they come for free.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{The Product Proof}

Suppose that the prover has committed to $m_1, m_2, m_3 \in \mathcal{R}_q$ and wants to prove that $m_1m_2 = m_3$ (we will explain later why operating over $\mathcal{R}_q$ is indeed fruitful).
The idea is to combine the masked openings into an expression in which the relation appears as a coefficient of a polynomial in $c$:
\begin{align*}
    f_1f_2 + c f_3 
    &= (a_1 - cm_1)(a_2 - cm_2) + c(a_3 - cm_3) \\
    &= \textcolor{oc-orange-8}{(m_1m_2 - m_3)}c^2 + \textcolor{oc-indigo-8}{(a_3 - a_1m_2 - a_2m_1)}c + a_1a_2.
\end{align*}
If the relation holds, the quadratic coefficient vanishes and the expression is \emph{linear} in $c$.
The remaining task is to let the verifier check linearity without learning anything about the messages, and ALS20 do this with a single \emph{garbage commitment}.
Before seeing $c$, the prover additionally commits to the linear coefficient $g := \textcolor{oc-indigo-8}{a_3 - a_1m_2 - a_2m_1}$ as a fourth message, $t_4 := \langle \mathbf{b}_4, \mathbf{r} \rangle + g$, and sends $v := a_1a_2 + a_4$ where $a_4 := \langle \mathbf{b}_4, \mathbf{y} \rangle$.
The masked opening of the garbage term is $f_4 = a_4 - cg$, so
\begin{equation*}
    f_1f_2 + cf_3 + f_4 = \textcolor{oc-orange-8}{(m_1m_2 - m_3)}c^2 + a_1a_2 + a_4 = \textcolor{oc-orange-8}{(m_1m_2 - m_3)}c^2 + v.
\end{equation*}
Hence, the verifier accepts if and only if $f_1f_2 + cf_3 + f_4 = v$.
The complete protocol is specified in \Cref{fig:als20-product}; the public parameters contain $\mathbf{b}_1, \dots, \mathbf{b}_4$, so that the prover can append the garbage commitment $t_4$ to $\mathsf{T} = (\mathbf{t}_0, t_1, t_2, t_3)$ under the same randomness $\mathbf{r}$.

    \small
    \centering

                     innerleftmargin=5pt, innerrightmargin=5pt]

            \colorbox{oc-blue-1}{$\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pp}, \mathbbm{x} = (\mathsf{T}), \mathbbm{w} = (m_1, m_2, m_3, \mathbf{r}))$:}

\subsubsection{Packing $\mathbb{Z}_q$ Values into NTT Slots}

So far, the protocol proves products of \emph{polynomials}, while applications need products of \emph{numbers} in $\mathbb{Z}_q$.
The ``bridge'' is the number-theoretic transform (NTT).
If $q \equiv 1 \pmod{2d}$, then $T^d+1$ splits into distinct linear factors modulo $q$,
\begin{equation*}
    T^d + 1 = \prod_{j=1}^{d}(T - \zeta_j) \pmod q,
\end{equation*}
where $\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_d \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ are the primitive $2d$-th roots of unity (recall from Exercise~6 in \Cref{section:lattices-exercises} that $T$ itself has order exactly $2d$ in $\mathcal{R}$; you may also recall some specifics of NTT computation from \Cref{section:ntt}, although here we state a more algebraic result).
By the Chinese remainder theorem, the map
\begin{equation*}
    \mathsf{NTT}: \mathcal{R}_q \to \mathbb{Z}_q^d, \quad m \mapsto \bigl(m(\zeta_1), \dots, m(\zeta_d)\bigr)
\end{equation*}
is a ring isomorphism, where $\mathbb{Z}_q^d$ is equipped with coordinate-wise addition and Hadamard product.
Each coordinate is an evaluation map $m \mapsto m(\zeta_j)$, which is a ring homomorphism precisely because $\zeta_j$ is a root of $T^d+1$ (this is Exercise~11 in \Cref{section:lattices-exercises} for $d = 2$ and $q = 17$).
We call the coordinates of $\mathsf{NTT}(m)$ the \textbf{NTT slots} of $m$, and we write $\mathsf{NTT}^{-1}$ for the inverse map.
In particular, $\mathsf{NTT}(m_1m_2) = \mathsf{NTT}(m_1) \odot \mathsf{NTT}(m_2)$, where $\odot$ is the Hadamard product; see \Cref{fig:ntt-packing}.

\subsubsection{Soundness in Fully Splitting Rings*}

The soundness argument above requires that the differences of challenges are invertible.
In a fully splitting ring, however, an element $\bar{c}$ is invertible if and only if \emph{all} of its NTT slots $\bar{c}(\zeta_j)$ are non-zero.
For a random short challenge, each slot $c(\zeta_j)$ is close to uniform over $\mathbb{Z}_q$, so a cheating prover can guess the value of a single slot of the challenge with probability about $1/q$.
In this case, the prover can cheat in the corresponding NTT slot: for example, it can commit to a value that is not a bit in one slot and still pass the product proof.
For $q \approx 2^{32}$, the soundness error $1/q$ is far from negligible.

Repeating the protocol $k$ times reduces the soundness error to $q^{-k}$, but it multiplies the number of masks and garbage terms.
ALS20 amplify soundness much more cheaply by using the automorphisms of $\mathcal{R}_q$.
Recall that for every odd $i$ the map $\varphi_i: T \mapsto T^i$ is an automorphism of $\mathcal{R}_q$, and it permutes the NTT slots.
Instead of $k$ independent challenges, the prover runs $k$ opening proofs in parallel with the challenges $c, \varphi(c), \dots, \varphi^{k-1}(c)$ for a single $c$ and a suitable automorphism $\varphi$ of order $k$.
Since $\varphi^i$ moves the slot that the prover guessed to a different slot, a single guess no longer suffices to cheat in all $k$ repetitions.
Moreover, the $k$ product relations are combined with random coefficients $\alpha_i \in \mathcal{R}_q$ into a single equation, so that only \emph{one} garbage commitment is needed.
The resulting soundness error is roughly $(3/q)^k$; see~\cite[Section 5]{C:AttLyuSei20} for more details.

\subsection{Proving Linear and Quadratic Relations over $\mathbb{Z}_q$}\label{section:lnp22}

The ALS20 product proof is efficient, but it has three limitations that become visible in larger applications.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textit{(i)}] Every committed message costs one element of $\mathcal{R}_q$ in the BDLOP commitment. Committing to a long witness, for example a vector with $2048$ ternary coefficients, is therefore expensive.
    \item[\textit{(ii)}] Packing values into NTT slots requires a fully splitting ring, which in turn requires the automorphism trick to amplify soundness.
    \item[\textit{(iii)}] Many natural statements are not products at all. For example, lattice-based primitives typically need to prove knowledge of $\mathbf{s}$ with $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{t}$ and $\|\mathbf{s}\|_2 \leq \beta$, \emph{exactly}, rather than the relaxed statement of \Cref{remark:relaxed-extraction}.
\end{enumerate}
Lyubashevsky, Nguyen, and Plan\c{c}on~\cite{C:LyuNguPla22} (LNP22) address all three.
Their framework proves arbitrary linear and quadratic relations over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ among committed values, including exact norm bounds, and it rests on four ideas that we present in turn: a combined commitment scheme, an encoding of $\mathbb{Z}_q$-relations into \emph{constant coefficients}, a quadratic proof in the style of ALS20, and an approximate range proof that lifts relations from $\mathbb{Z}_q$ to $\mathbb{Z}$.

\subsubsection{The ABDLOP Commitment Scheme}

Notice that Ajtai and BDLOP commitments make opposite trade-offs.
The Ajtai commitment supports arbitrarily long messages at no extra cost, but only short ones: the message dimension does not enter the commitment size, and MSIS hardness is essentially independent of the number of columns.
The BDLOP commitment supports messages with arbitrary coefficients, but each of them costs one element of $\mathcal{R}_q$.
The ABDLOP commitment scheme of~\cite{C:LyuNguPla22} merges the two constructions under a single randomness $\boldsymbol{\rho}$.
Short messages go into the Ajtai part, where their dimension does not affect the commitment size, and messages with unrestricted coefficients go into the BDLOP part.

\begin{definition}[ABDLOP Commitment]\label{def:abdlop}
    Define the \textbf{ABDLOP commitment scheme} over the message space $S_{\alpha}^m \times \mathcal{R}_q^n$ (short messages are denoted by $\mathbf{x}$ and long ones by $\mathbf{m}$) as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda}) \to \mathsf{pp}$: sample $\mathbf{A} \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^{\kappa \times m}$, $\mathbf{A}_{\rho} \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^{\kappa \times r}$, and $\mathbf{B} \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^{n \times r}$; output $\mathsf{pp} \gets (\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}_{\rho}, \mathbf{B})$.
        \item $\mathsf{Com}(\mathsf{pp}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}) \to (\mathsf{T}, \boldsymbol{\rho})$: choose a short random vector $\boldsymbol{\rho} \leftarrowS \chi^r$ and compute
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbf{t}_A \gets \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{A}_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\rho}, \quad \mathbf{t}_B \gets \mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\rho} + \mathbf{m}.
        \end{equation*}
        Let $\mathsf{T} \gets (\mathbf{t}_A, \mathbf{t}_B)$ and output $(\mathsf{T}, \boldsymbol{\rho})$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Some properties} The proofs of binding and hiding are similar to those of Ajtai and BDLOP commitments; we recommend the reader doing it nonetheless.

The main advantage of ABDLOP over BDLOP is the cost of committing to $\mathbf{x}$.
In BDLOP, the top part $\mathbf{t}_0 = \mathbf{B}_0\boldsymbol{\rho}$ carries no message and only binds the randomness.
ABDLOP reuses these $\kappa$ ring elements to also commit to $\mathbf{x}$, so the commitment size does not depend on the length $m$ of $\mathbf{x}$ at all, while each element of $\mathbf{m}$ costs one ring element of $\mathbf{t}_B$.
Hence, one puts the (long) short witness into $\mathbf{x}$ and only a few auxiliary values into $\mathbf{m}$.
One can also append new elements to the BDLOP part later by publishing extra rows: for a fresh public matrix $\mathbf{B}_g$, the value $\mathbf{t}_g := \mathbf{B}_g\boldsymbol{\rho} + \mathbf{g}$ commits to additional messages $\mathbf{g}$ under the same randomness, which is what the protocols below do with their auxiliary terms.

\paragraph{Opening proof with linear constraints}
Given an ABDLOP commitment $\mathsf{T} = (\mathbf{t}_A, \mathbf{t}_B)$, we first want to prove knowledge of the opening together with public $\mathcal{R}_q$-linear relations on the committed messages.
Naturally, $N$ \emph{linear relations} over witness $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{m})$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{L}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{R}\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{s}
\end{equation*}
for some fixed matrices $\mathbf{L} \in \mathcal{R}_q^{N \times m}$, $\mathbf{R} \in \mathcal{R}_q^{N \times n}$, and vector $\mathbf{s} \in \mathcal{R}_q^{N}$.
The proof is again the Lyubashevsky protocol, now with two secret vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{\rho}$.
As in ALS20, the verifier can compute a masked opening of $\mathbf{m}$ from public data: $c\mathbf{t}_B - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}_{\rho} = c\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}_{\rho}$.
This allows the prover to add the linear relations at no extra cost: it sends $\mathbf{v} := \mathbf{L}\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{R}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}_{\rho}$, and the verifier checks the last equation in \Cref{fig:lnp22-linear}.

The challenges are short polynomials that are fixed by the automorphism $T \mapsto T^{-1}$, for reasons that will become clear shortly.
For this ring, every non-zero element fixed by $T \mapsto T^{-1}$ is invertible, so the difference of any two distinct challenges is invertible, and no soundness amplification is needed.

\subsubsection{From $\mathcal{R}_q$ to $\mathbb{Z}_q$: Constant Coefficients}

Instead of packing values of $\mathbb{Z}_q$ into NTT slots, LNP22 pack them into the \emph{coefficients} of polynomials and use the following observation.
For $f \in \mathcal{R}_q$, write $f^{\ast} := f(T^{-1})$ for its \textbf{conjugate}, which is the image of $f$ under the \emph{conjugation automorphism} $T \mapsto T^{-1}$, and $\mathsf{ct}(f) := f_0$ for its \textbf{constant coefficient}.
Recall from Exercise~6 in \Cref{section:lattices-exercises} that $T$ is invertible with $T^{-1} = -T^{d-1}$, so $f^{\ast}$ is again an element of $\mathcal{R}_q$.
For vectors in $\mathcal{R}_q^n$, the conjugate is applied component-wise.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:constant-coefficient}
    The following two facts are true:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item[\textit{(i)}] let $f := \sum_{i<d} f_iT^i$ and $g := \sum_{i<d} g_iT^i$; then $\mathsf{ct}(f^{\ast}g) = \sum_{i<d} f_ig_i$;
        \item[\textit{(ii)}] let $\mathbf{f} = (f_1,\dots,f_n)$ and $\mathbf{g} = (g_1,\dots,g_n)$ be vectors in $\mathcal{R}_q^n$ with $f_i = \sum_{j<d} f_{i,j}T^j$ and $g_i = \sum_{j<d} g_{i,j}T^j$; then $\mathsf{ct}(\mathbf{f}^{\ast\top}\mathbf{g}) = \sum_{i,j} f_{i,j}g_{i,j}$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We have $f^{\ast}g = \sum_{i,j} f_ig_jT^{j-i}$.
    For $0 \leq i,j < d$, the monomial $T^{j-i}$ equals $\pm T^{k}$ for some $k>0$ unless $i = j$, so only the terms with $i = j$ contribute to the constant coefficient.
    The second fact follows by summing the first over all components.
\end{proof}

The special case $f = g$ is exactly Exercise~10 in \Cref{section:lattices-exercises}: the constant coefficient of $f^{\ast}f$ is $\|f\|_2^2$.
The lemma turns every $\mathbb{Z}_q$-linear or $\mathbb{Z}_q$-quadratic relation among the coefficients of committed polynomials into the statement that one constant coefficient of an $\mathcal{R}_q$-expression vanishes.
Let us consider the following examples.
\begin{example}\label{ex:linear-ct-constraints}
    Here is a couple of examples how we can encode statements over $\mathbb{Z}_q^d$ using just a couple of polynomials in $\mathcal{R}_q$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Suppose we want to show that $\sum_{i \in [d]}a_ix_i = b$ for some fixed constants $a_0,\dots,a_{d-1},b \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ and witness $x_0,\dots,x_{d-1} \in \mathbb{Z}_q$.
        To convert this check over cyclotomic ring $\mathcal{R}_q$, one introduces two polynomials: $a := \sum_{i \in [d]}a_iT^i$, $x := \sum_{i \in [d]} x_iT^i$, and requires $\mathsf{ct}(a^*x-b) = 0$.
        \item Suppose that for the given witness $x_0,\dots,x_{d-1} \in \mathbb{Z}_q$ we want to verify that $x_i=0$ for all $i>\nu$. 
        One again lets $x := \sum_{i \in [d]}x_iT^i$ and enforces multiple constraints $\mathsf{ct}((T^i)^*x)=0$ for all $i>\nu$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

In both cases, the statement has the same shape.
There are public $\mathcal{R}_q$-affine functions $F_1, \dots, F_M$ of the committed messages, and we need to show that
\begin{equation*}
    \mathsf{ct}(F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m})) = 0 \quad \text{for all} \; j \in [M],
\end{equation*}
where $F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}) = a^{\ast}x - b$ in the first example and $F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}) = (T^{\nu+j})^{\ast}x$ in the second.
Quadratic relations lead to the same shape with quadratic $F_j$, which we treat in the next subsection.

\paragraph{Proving a constant coefficient}
Unfortunately, we cannot directly employ \Cref{fig:lnp22-linear} to prove linear statements obtained from \Cref{lemma:constant-coefficient} (for instance those specified in \Cref{ex:linear-ct-constraints}).
Indeed, this algorithm can only prove that the linear relation between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{m}$ vanishes, but it states nothing about coefficients separately.
So our goal is to be able to prove a set of $M$ statements $\mathsf{ct}(F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m})) = 0$ where $F_j$'s are $\mathcal{R}_q$-linear (affine) functions. 

Consider first a single function $F$.
If the prover simply revealed the polynomial $F(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m})$, the verifier could check its constant coefficient, but the remaining $d-1$ coefficients would leak information about the witness.
The idea of LNP22 is to hide these coefficients with a \textbf{mask}: a committed polynomial $g$ whose constant coefficient is zero and whose other coefficients are uniformly random.
The prover then reveals $h := g + F(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m})$ and proves with \Cref{fig:lnp22-linear} that $h$ is computed correctly, which is now an ordinary linear relation among the committed $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{m}$, $g$ with a public right-hand side $h$.
Since $\mathsf{ct}(h) = \mathsf{ct}(F(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}))$, the verifier learns exactly whether the constant coefficient vanishes, and nothing else.

Now suppose we have multiple statements. Applying this to each $F_j$ separately would cost $M$ masks, and hence $M$ extra commitment slots.
Instead, the prover commits to only $k \ll M$ masks $g_1, \dots, g_k$. After that, the verifier sends random $\gamma_{i,j} \leftarrowS \mathbb{Z}_q$, and the prover combines all functions into $k$ random linear combinations:
\begin{equation*}
    h_i := g_i + \sum_{j \in [M]} \gamma_{i,j}F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}), \quad i \in [k].
\end{equation*}
The verifier checks that $\mathsf{ct}(h_i) = 0$ for all $i \in [k]$, and the prover shows with \Cref{fig:lnp22-linear} that each $h_i$ is computed correctly, as before.

Why does it work though? Suppose $\mathsf{ct}(F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m})) \neq 0$ for some $j$. Then $\mathsf{ct}(h_i) = \sum_j \gamma_{i,j}\,\mathsf{ct}(F_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}))$ is uniformly random over the choice of $\gamma_{i,\cdot}$, since $g_i$ was committed before the challenges, so it vanishes with probability $1/q$.
Hence, $k$ independent combinations give soundness error $q^{-k}$.
The protocol is specified in \Cref{fig:lnp22-ct}.

\subsubsection{Quadratic Relations}

It remains to prove \emph{quadratic} relations since for now we only considered linear relations.
These relations involve both the committed messages and their conjugates (thus enabling using the powerful results of \Cref{lemma:constant-coefficient}), so we collect them into the so-called \emph{extended witness}
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{x}_{\varphi} := \varphi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{m}) := (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}^{\ast}, \mathbf{m}, \mathbf{m}^{\ast}).
\end{equation*}
Recall that an arbitrary quadratic function of $\mathbf{x}_{\varphi}$ can be expressed as follows: $f(\mathbf{x}_{\varphi}) = \mathbf{x}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}_{\varphi} + \langle \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{x}_{\varphi} \rangle + p_0$; our goal is to prove that $f(\mathbf{x}_{\varphi}) = 0$.

The approach is the one of ALS20: build a masked opening of $\mathbf{x}_{\varphi}$ that is affine in $c$, evaluate $f$ on it, and cancel the linear term with a garbage commitment along the way.
From the opening proof, the verifier knows the masked openings $\mathbf{z} = c\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}$ and that $c\mathbf{t}_B - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}_{\rho} = c\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}_{\rho}$.
Taking conjugates gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{z}^{\ast} = c^{\ast}\mathbf{x}^{\ast} + \mathbf{y}^{\ast},
\end{equation*}
which is a masked opening of $\mathbf{x}^{\ast}$ with the \emph{same} challenge only if $c^{\ast} = c$.
This is why LNP22 restrict the challenges to polynomials fixed by conjugation.
With such challenges, the verifier can compute
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{z}_{\varphi} := \varphi\bigl(\mathbf{z}, c\mathbf{t}_B - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}_{\rho}\bigr) = c\mathbf{x}_{\varphi} + \mathbf{y}_{\varphi} \; \text{where} \; \mathbf{y}_{\varphi} := \varphi(\mathbf{y}, -\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}_{\rho}).
\end{equation*}
Substituting $\mathbf{z}_{\varphi} = c\mathbf{x}_{\varphi} + \mathbf{y}_{\varphi}$ into the quadratic function gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{z}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}_{\varphi} + c\langle \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{z}_{\varphi} \rangle + c^2p_0 = \textcolor{oc-orange-8}{f(\mathbf{x}_{\varphi})}c^2 + \textcolor{oc-teal-8}{a_1}c + a_0
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        a_1 &:= \mathbf{x}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}_{\varphi} + \mathbf{y}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}_{\varphi} + \langle \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{y}_{\varphi} \rangle, \\
        a_0 &:= \mathbf{y}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}_{\varphi}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
As in ALS20, the prover commits to the linear coefficient in the BDLOP part, $t := \langle \mathbf{b}, \boldsymbol{\rho} \rangle + \textcolor{oc-teal-8}{a_1}$ for a public $\mathbf{b} \leftarrowS \mathcal{R}_q^r$, and masks the constant term as $v := \langle \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{y}_{\rho} \rangle + a_0$.
The verifier computes the masked opening $ct - \langle \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{z}_{\rho} \rangle = ca_1 - \langle \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{y}_{\rho} \rangle$ of the garbage term and checks that
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{z}_{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{z}_{\varphi} + c\langle \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{z}_{\varphi} \rangle + c^2p_0 - \bigl(ct - \langle \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{z}_{\rho} \rangle\bigr) = v,
\end{equation*}
which holds exactly when the quadratic coefficient $\textcolor{oc-orange-8}{f(\mathbf{x}_{\varphi})}$ vanishes.
The protocol is specified in \Cref{fig:lnp22-quadratic}.

\subsubsection{Lifting from $\mathbb{Z}_q$ to $\mathbb{Z}$: Approximate Range Proofs}

All the proofs above certify relations modulo $q$, while norm and range statements are about integers.
To see why this matters, suppose we want to prove that some integer $u$ lies in range $[0,2^{\tau})$.
Here is how we may do it: let the binary decomposition be $u = \sum_{i<\tau} u_i2^i$; we encode this binary decomposition into the \emph{bit polynomial} $u(T) := \sum_{i<\tau} u_iT^i$. 
Introduce the following helper (publicly computable) gadget polynomials:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathsf{g}_1 := \sum_{i<\tau} T^i, \qquad \mathsf{g}_2 := \sum_{i<\tau} 2^iT^i.
\end{equation*}
For a polynomial $u$ with integer coefficients $u_0, \dots, u_{d-1}$, \Cref{lemma:constant-coefficient} gives
\begin{equation*}
    \mathsf{ct}\bigl(u^{\ast}(u - \mathsf{g}_1)\bigr) = \sum_{i < \tau} u_i(u_i - 1) + \sum_{i \geq \tau} u_i^2, \; \mathsf{ct}(\mathsf{g}_2^{\ast}u) = \sum_{i<\tau} 2^iu_i = u(2).
\end{equation*}

Notice that the second statement is nothing but an original integer $u$ computed from the corresponding bit polynomial.
For the first sum, over $\mathbb{Z}$, every term is non-negative, so it vanishes if and only if $u_i \in \{0,1\}$ for $i < \tau$ and $u_i = 0$ for $i \geq \tau$, \textit{i.e.}, if and only if $u$ is a bit polynomial.
So a single constant coefficient certifies \emph{both} binarity and vanishing above $\tau$, and the range $u(2) \in [0, 2^{\tau})$ is a consequence of binarity.

Over $\mathbb{Z}_q$, however, the first sum may be a non-zero multiple of $q$, for example when $u$ has large coefficients.
The relation holds over $\mathbb{Z}$ as soon as we additionally know that $\|\mathbf{u}\|_2$ is small enough for the sum to stay below $q$.

LNP22 obtain such a bound with an \emph{approximate range proof} based on random projections.
Further let $\mathcal{B}$ denote the distribution on $\{0, \pm 1\}$ with $\Pr[0] = 1/2$ and $\Pr[\pm 1] = 1/4$.

\begin{lemma}[{\cite[Lemma 2.9]{C:LyuNguPla22}}]\label{lemma:jl-projection}
    Fix $m$ and $B \leq q/41m$.
    Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^m$ with $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \geq B$, and let $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{256}$ be \emph{arbitrary}.
    Then, for $\mathbf{R} \leftarrowS \mathcal{B}^{256 \times m}$, one has $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y} \bmod q\|_2 < \frac{1}{2}B\sqrt{26}$ with probability less than $2^{-128}$.
\end{lemma}

The mask $\mathbf{y}$ is quantified over without any norm restriction, and this is what makes the lemma usable.
The prover commits to a Gaussian mask $\mathbf{y}_B \leftarrowS \dgaussian_{\sigma_B}^{256}$ in the BDLOP part, receives $\mathbf{R} \leftarrowS \mathcal{B}^{256 \times md}$ from the verifier, and responds with $\mathbf{z}_B := \mathbf{R}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}_B$, where rejection sampling removes the dependence on $\mathbf{x}$.
It then proves, with $256$ constant-coefficient statements, that $\mathbf{z}_B$ was computed correctly, and the verifier checks that $\|\mathbf{z}_B\|_2$ is small.
By \Cref{lemma:jl-projection}, a prover who reveals a masked $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}$ of small norm cannot have a long $\mathbf{x}$, whatever mask it used.
The proof is only approximate (the certified bound is a constant factor larger than the true norm), but this suffices to rule out wraparound modulo $q$, and it costs only $256$ integers and one rejection step.
