\subsection{The FRI Protocol}\label{subsec:fri}

In \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}, the whole compilation of a Poly-IOP into an IOP was based on a single black box: a $\delta$-RS-IOPP (\Cref{def:rs-proximity}) that tests whether one function $h: \Omega \to \mathbb{F}$ is close to $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega, k]$.
In this subsection, we open this ``box''.

Recall why the problem is not trivial.
Without the help of the prover, the verifier needs $k + 1$ queries: it interpolates $h$ through $k$ points and checks the result at one more point.
In applications, $k$ is the size of the whole computation, so such a verifier is as slow as recomputing everything.
The \emph{Fast Reed-Solomon IOP of Proximity} (FRI)~\cite{bensasson18} lets the prover send a few additional oracles, after which the verifier needs only $O(\log n)$ queries.

In fact, we already have the main ingredient.
By \Cref{lemma:two-way-folding}, folding maps the problem ``is $g$ close to $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega, k]$?'' to the same problem for $\mathsf{Fold}(g, r)$, a code of half the dimension, and a domain of half the size.
FRI applies it again and again: after $\log_2 k$ folds the dimension drops to $1$, the code consists of constant functions, and the prover simply sends the constant.
Throughout this subsection, we keep the setting of \Cref{lemma:two-way-folding} and assume that $k = 2^\ell$.
To avoid confusion with the evaluation points $r_i$ of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}, we denote the folding challenges by $\beta$.

\subsubsection{Folding Is Local}\label{subsubsec:fri-folding}

There is one obstacle: the verifier cannot compute $\mathsf{Fold}(g, \beta)$ itself, since that would require reading all of $g$.
Instead, the prover will send the fold as a new oracle, and the verifier will spot-check it.
This works because folding is \emph{local}: by \Cref{def:fold}, the value of $\mathsf{Fold}(g, \beta)$ at $x^2$ depends only on the two values $g(x)$ and $g(-x)$.
So to check one value of the fold, the verifier reads just two cells of $g$.
There is also a pleasant geometric way to see this, which is how folding is described in~\cite{bensasson18} and illustrated in \Cref{fig:fri-fold-line}.

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:fri-line}
    Let $L$ be the line through the points $(x, g(x))$ and $(-x, g(-x))$, that is, the unique polynomial of degree at most $1$ with $L(x) = g(x)$ and $L(-x) = g(-x)$.
    Show that $\mathsf{Fold}(g, \beta)(x^2) = L(\beta)$.
\end{exercise}

Let us consider some concrete example.

\begin{example}\label{ex:fri-fold}
    Let $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{F}_{17}$, $\Omega = \langle 2 \rangle$ and $k = 4$, so that $\rho = 1/2$.
    Listing $\Omega$ as $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 15, 13, 9$ (the powers of $\omega = 2$), the polynomial $f = 3 + T + 2T^2 + 5T^3$ gives the codeword
    \begin{equation*}
        g = \overline{f} = (11, 2, 2, 13, 16, 3, 0, 11).
    \end{equation*}
    Take $\beta = 2$, and recall the circle of \Cref{fig:fri-domains}.
    The first entry of the fold, at $1^2 = 1$, uses the pair $\{1, 16\} = \{1, -1\}$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \mathsf{Fold}(g, 2)(1) = \frac{11 + 16}{2} + 2 \cdot \frac{11 - 16}{2} = 27 \cdot 9 - 5 = 0 \pmod{17},
    \end{equation*}
    where we used $2^{-1} = 9$ in $\mathbb{F}_{17}$.
    Doing the same for the other three pairs gives $\mathsf{Fold}(g, 2) = (0, 2, 10, 8)$ on $\Omega^2$, listed as $1, 4, 16, 13$.
    As expected, these are the evaluations of $f_E + 2 f_O = 5 + 12T$, a polynomial of degree less than $2$.
\end{example}

\subsubsection{The Protocol}\label{subsubsec:fri-protocol}

To fold $\ell$ times, write $\Omega_i := \Omega^{2^i}$ for the domain after $i$ folds (so $\Omega_0 = \Omega$ and $\Omega_1 = \Omega^2$), and $\code_i := \mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega_i, k/2^i]$ for the corresponding code; all these codes have the same rate $\rho$, and the last one, $\code_\ell$, consists of the constant functions.
The prover sends every fold as a new oracle, and the verifier spot-checks consecutive oracles.
As in~\cite{bensasson18}, this happens in two phases: in the \emph{commit phase}, the prover sends all oracles, and only afterwards, in the \emph{query phase}, the verifier makes its queries.

\begin{definition}[FRI]\label{def:fri}
    The input is an oracle $g_0 := g: \Omega_0 \to \mathbb{F}$, and the goal is to test proximity to $\code_0 = \mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega, k]$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Commit phase.} For every $i \in [\ell]$:
        \begin{algoen}
            \item the verifier samples $\beta_i \leftarrowS \mathbb{F}$;
            \item the prover sends an oracle $g_{i+1}: \Omega_{i+1} \to \mathbb{F}$; an honest prover sets $g_{i+1} := \mathsf{Fold}(g_i, \beta_i)$.
        \end{algoen}
        Finally, the prover sends a constant $c \in \mathbb{F}$, and we set $g_\ell(y) := c$ for all $y \in \Omega_\ell$.
        \item \textbf{Query phase.} Repeat $s$ times:
        \begin{algoen}
            \item the verifier samples $x_0 \leftarrowS \Omega_0$ and sets $x_{i+1} := x_i^2$ for every $i \in [\ell]$;
            \item for every $i \in [\ell]$, the verifier queries $g_i(x_i)$ and $g_i(-x_i)$ and checks the \emph{round consistency}
            \begin{equation*}
                g_{i+1}(x_{i+1}) = \frac{g_i(x_i) + g_i(-x_i)}{2} + \beta_i \cdot \frac{g_i(x_i) - g_i(-x_i)}{2x_i}.
            \end{equation*}
        \end{algoen}
    \end{itemize}
    The verifier accepts if and only if all checks pass.
\end{definition}

Note that the left-hand side of the round consistency check needs no extra query: $g_{i+1}(x_{i+1})$ is one of the two values queried in the next round, and in the last round it is the constant $c$.
The whole protocol is depicted in \Cref{fig:fri-protocol}, and \Cref{fig:fri-tree} below shows the same run as a tree.
By the first claim of \Cref{lemma:two-way-folding}, an honest prover is always accepted: every $g_i$ is a codeword of $\code_i$, so $g_\ell$ is indeed constant.

\begin{example}\label{ex:fri-run}
    We continue \Cref{ex:fri-fold}, where $k = 4$, so FRI has $\ell = 2$ rounds.
    After $\beta_0 = 2$, the honest prover sends $g_1 = (0, 2, 10, 8)$ on $\Omega_1 = \{1, 4, 16, 13\}$.
    After $\beta_1 = 5$, it folds $g_1$ once more and obtains $g_2 = (14, 14)$ on $\Omega_2 = \{1, 16\}$, which is indeed constant, so it sends $c = 14$.
    If the verifier samples $x_0 = 2$, then $x_1 = 4$ and it checks
    \begin{equation*}
        g_1(4) = \frac{g_0(2) + g_0(15)}{2} + 2 \cdot \frac{g_0(2) - g_0(15)}{2 \cdot 2} = \frac{2 + 3}{2} + \frac{2 - 3}{2} = 2,
    \end{equation*}
    and similarly $c = g_1(4)/2 + g_1(13)/2 + 5 \cdot (g_1(4) - g_1(13))/(2 \cdot 4) = 14$.
    The whole run is shown in \Cref{fig:fri-tree}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:fri-cheat}
    Now let the prover try to pass off the evaluations of $T^5$,
    \begin{equation*}
        g_0 = \overline{T^5} = (1, 15, 4, 9, 16, 2, 13, 8),
    \end{equation*}
    which is not a codeword of $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}_{17}, \Omega, 4]$.
    Suppose it folds honestly, with the same challenges $\beta_0 = 2$ and $\beta_1 = 5$.
    Since $T^5 = T \cdot (T^2)^2$, we get $g_E = 0$ and $g_O = \overline{T^2}$, so $g_1 = \overline{2T^2} = (2, 15, 2, 15)$: the excess degree survived the fold.
    Folding again gives $g_2 = \overline{2T} = (2, 15)$ on $\Omega_2 = \{1, -1\}$, which is not constant.
    Whatever constant $c$ the prover sends, it differs from $g_2$ at one of the two points, and each query catches this with probability $1/2$.
    The prover could instead send a $g_1$ that \emph{is} a codeword, but then $g_1$ is inconsistent with $g_0$ on many pairs, and the first round consistency check catches it.
\end{example}

\subsubsection{Efficiency and Soundness}\label{subsubsec:fri-soundness}

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
The prover computes each fold in time linear in the size of the current domain, so its total work is $O(n + n/2 + \dots) = O(n)$ field operations.
The oracles $g_1, \dots, g_{\ell-1}$ have total length $n/2 + n/4 + \dots < n$, so the proof length is less than the length of the input.
Every repetition of the query phase reads $2$ values in each of the $\ell$ rounds, so the query complexity is $2\ell s = O(s \log n)$, and the verifier's work is of the same order.

\paragraph{Soundness.}
In the commit phase, by the second claim of \Cref{lemma:two-way-folding}, every round keeps a far function far, except with probability $\varepsilon_\delta$ over $\beta_i$.
If the prover sends an oracle $g_{i+1}$ that is close to the code, then $g_{i+1}$ must differ from the true fold of $g_i$ on many points, and the round consistency check detects this at a random point.
Either way, a far input leaves a trace of inconsistencies of density about $\delta$ along the path of a random query.
The following theorem makes this intuition precise.

\begin{theorem}[FRI soundness, informal]\label{thm:fri-soundness}
    Let $g_0$ be $\delta$-far from $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega, k]$.
    Then for every prover, the FRI verifier with $s$ repetitions accepts with probability at most
    \begin{equation*}
        \ell \cdot \varepsilon_\delta + (1 - \delta)^s,
    \end{equation*}
    for every $\delta$ below the Johnson bound $1 - \sqrt{\rho}$ (with $\varepsilon_\delta$ as in \Cref{lemma:proximity-gap-simplified})~\cite{FOCS:BCIKS20}.
\end{theorem}

The first term is the probability that some challenge $\beta_i$ is unlucky, and it is negligible for a large field.
The second term is the probability that all $s$ query paths miss the inconsistencies, and it is what determines the number of queries in practice.

\begin{remark}
    The original analysis in~\cite[Theorem~3.3]{bensasson18} gives a weaker bound: the verifier accepts with probability at most $3n/\#\mathbb{F} + (1 - \min\{\delta, \delta_0\})^s$, where $\delta_0 \approx (1 - 3\rho)/4$ lies below the unique decoding radius.
    The improvement to the Johnson bound came later, first with DEEP-FRI~\cite{ITCS:BGKS20} % TODO: bib key
    and then with the proximity gap theorem of~\cite{FOCS:BCIKS20}.
    Already~\cite[Conjecture~1.5]{bensasson18} conjectured that the bound holds up to the capacity $1 - \rho$; this is the conjecture discussed in \Cref{subsubsec:list-decoding}.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{FRI in the Big Picture}\label{subsubsec:fri-big-picture}

Let us now return to \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}.
There, the input of the RS-IOPP is the virtual function $h = \sum_j \alpha^j \mathsf{q}_j$, which the prover never commits to.
FRI handles this without any change: whenever the verifier needs $h(\pm x_0)$ in the first round, it queries every committed oracle of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt} at $\pm x_0$ and computes $h(\pm x_0)$ itself.
So the first layer of FRI comes for free: only the folds $g_1, \dots, g_{\ell-1}$ and the constant $c$ are new messages of the prover.

What remains is to turn the IOP into a SNARK.
This is done by the transformation of Ben-Sasson, Chiesa and Spooner~\cite{TCC:BenChiSpo16}:
\begin{algoen}
    \item every oracle is replaced by the root of a Merkle tree (\Cref{section:commitment-schemes}), and every query is answered with an authentication path;
    \item every challenge, including the query points $x_0$, is derived by hashing the transcript so far (\Cref{subsection:fiat-shamir}).
\end{algoen}
Both steps need only a hash function, so the resulting proof system needs no trusted setup and is plausibly post-quantum.
\Cref{fig:fri-pipeline} summarizes the whole path, which is the skeleton of every STARK.

\subsubsection{Variants and Newer Protocols}\label{subsubsec:fri-variants}

Of course the protocol of \Cref{def:fri} is the simplest approach that has seen numerous improvements since the first introduction.
The most common change is the \emph{folding factor}.
Instead of pairs $\{x, -x\}$, one can fold whole cosets of size $2^\eta$ at once, using the map $x \mapsto x^{2^\eta}$ and reducing the degree by a factor of $2^\eta$ in one round.
The fold is then the value at $\beta_i$ of the polynomial of degree less than $2^\eta$ that interpolates $g_i$ on the coset, exactly as in \Cref{ex:fri-line}, which was the case $\eta = 1$.
\Cref{fig:fri-folding-factor} shows the benefit: a query reads slightly more values, but they all sit in one Merkle leaf, so far fewer authentication paths are sent.
The original paper uses $\eta = 2$~\cite{bensasson18}, and implementations typically use $\eta \in \{2, 3, 4\}$.
Another common change is to stop folding early, before reaching a constant, and to send the remaining polynomial of small degree in full, as the original paper does.

The domains may also differ.
The original FRI is stated for binary fields, where squaring is not two-to-one; there, the domains are additive cosets, and the map $x \mapsto x^2$ is replaced by a polynomial that vanishes on a small subspace~\cite{bensasson18}.
Some approaches take even a step further: for example, Circle STARKs~\cite{EPRINT:HabLevPap24}
work over $p = 2^{31} - 1$, where $p - 1$ has no large power of two as a factor, and use the points of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ instead, a group of the smooth order $p + 1 = 2^{31}$.

Finally, FRI is no longer the only RS-IOPP in use.
STIR~\cite{C:ACFY24}
lowers the rate in every round in addition to the degree, so that later rounds need fewer queries, and it achieves this by performing the out-of-domain sampling and quotienting of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-second-attempt} inside every round.
WHIR~\cite{EC:ACFY25}
and Basefold~\cite{C:ZeiCheFis24}
carry the same ideas over to multilinear polynomials, which combines our considered approach with sum-check-based Poly-IOPs of \Cref{section:sumcheck}.

\subsection{Arithmetic Intermediate Representation (AIR)}\label{subsec:air}

By now, we have a complete, hash-based compiler: \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt} turns any Poly-IOP into an IOP with a single run of FRI, and the BCS transformation (\Cref{fig:fri-pipeline}) turns this IOP into a SNARK.
What is still missing is the Poly-IOP itself: a way to express the statement ``this computation was performed correctly'' as claims about polynomials.
In \Cref{section:plonk}, PlonK did this for arithmetic circuits.
STARKs use a different arithmetization, tailored to computations that repeat the same step many times, such as the execution of a virtual machine or the rounds of a hash function.
It is called the \emph{arithmetic intermediate representation} (AIR).

Similar to other SNARKs, the STARK proof system thus has intermediate stages: the computation is first expressed as an \emph{arithmetic constraint system} (the AIR), which is then turned into a Poly-IOP, compiled into an IOP, and finally into a SNARK.

\begin{definition}\label{def:air}
  The \emph{arithmetic intermediate representation (AIR)} is a way of describing a computation in terms of an execution trace that satisfies a number of constraints induced by the correct evolution of the state.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:aet}
  Let $\mathbb{F}$ be the field of definition, and suppose the computation describes the evolution of a state of $w$ registers for $\tau$ cycles.
  We define the \emph{algebraic execution trace (AET)} as the table $W \in \mathbb{F}^{\tau \times w}$ where every row describes the state of the system at the given point in time, and every column tracks the value of the given register.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:st-function}
  A \emph{state transition function} $\phi: \mathbb{F}^{w} \to \mathbb{F}^{w}$ determines the state at the next cycle as a function of the state at the previous cycle.
  Furthermore, a set of \emph{boundary conditions}
  \begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{B} \subseteq [\tau] \times [w] \times \mathbb{F}
  \end{equation*}
  fixes the values of some registers at some cycles: a triple $(i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}$ means that register $j$ holds the value $v$ at cycle $i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-1}
  Consider a toy computation of the Fibonacci square sequence $a_{i+2} = a_{i+1}^2 + a_i^2$ over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$, with $a_0 = 1$ and $a_1 = s$.
  The prover claims that he knows $s$ such that $a_4 = 13$.
  Indeed, $s = 3$ works, since $a_2 = 1^2 + 3^2 = 10$, $a_3 = 3^2 + 10^2 = 109 = 7$ and $a_4 = 10^2 + 7^2 = 149 = 13$.

  We use $w = 2$ registers and $\tau = 4$ cycles: at cycle $i$, the registers hold two consecutive elements $a_i$ and $a_{i+1}$, so that $W_{[i,0]} = a_i$ and $W_{[i,1]} = a_{i+1}$.
  The algebraic execution trace is shown below.

\end{example}

The \emph{computational integrity claim} consists of the state transition function and the boundary conditions.
The \emph{witness} to this claim is the algebraic execution trace.
The claim is \emph{true} if there is a witness $W \in \mathbb{F}^{\tau \times w}$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item for every cycle, the state evolves correctly: that is, the condition $\phi(W_{[i, :]}) = W_{[i+1, :]}$ holds for all $i \in [\tau - 1]$;
  \item all boundary conditions are satisfied: $W_{[i, j]} = v$ for all $(i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}$.
\end{itemize}
Actually, the state transition function hides a lot of complexity, since it must be describable by low-degree polynomials that are independent of the cycle.
However, these polynomials do not need to compute the next state from the current one; they merely need to distinguish correct evolutions from incorrect ones.
Specifically, the state transition function $\phi$ is represented by a list of polynomials $p_u(X_0, \ldots, X_{w-1}, Y_0, \ldots, Y_{w-1})$, $u \in [\kappa]$, such that $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $p_u(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = 0$ for all $u \in [\kappa]$.
We call them \emph{transition verification polynomials}.
Then the transition constraints become: for all $i \in [\tau - 1]$ and $u \in [\kappa]$,
\begin{equation*}
  p_u(W_{[i, 0]}, \ldots, W_{[i, w-1]}, W_{[i+1, 0]}, \ldots, W_{[i+1, w-1]}) = 0.
\end{equation*}

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-2}
  We continue \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-1}.
  The state transition function is verified by $\kappa = 2$ polynomials
  \begin{equation*}
    p_0 = Y_0 - X_1, \qquad p_1 = Y_1 - X_0^2 - X_1^2.
  \end{equation*}
  The first one says that $a_{i+1}$ is copied to the next row, and the second one is the recurrence itself.
  We can check that both transition constraints are satisfied for the witness $W$ at cycle $i = 0$:
  \begin{align*}
    p_0(W_{[0, 0]}, W_{[0, 1]}, W_{[1, 0]}, W_{[1, 1]}) &= 3 - 3 = 0, \\
    p_1(W_{[0, 0]}, W_{[0, 1]}, W_{[1, 0]}, W_{[1, 1]}) &= 10 - 1^2 - 3^2 = 0.
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

This representation admits non-determinism: the verification polynomials may have much lower degree than any polynomial that computes the next state.
For instance, the transition $y = x^{-1}$ requires computing $x^{\#\mathbb{F} - 2}$, but it is verified by the degree-$2$ polynomial $XY - 1$.

\begin{remark}
  It is instructive to compare AIR with the arithmetic circuits of \Cref{section:r1cs}.
  A circuit can describe an arbitrary computation, but every gate needs its own constraint.
  An AIR describes only computations that repeat one step, but the same $\kappa$ polynomials describe all $\tau - 1$ steps, so the description of the computation does not grow with $\tau$.
  As we see next, this uniformity matches the cyclic structure of the evaluation domains perfectly: moving to the next row will become multiplication of the argument by a root of unity.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Trace Interpolation}

We saw that the arithmetic constraint system described above represents the computational integrity claim by a list of polynomials, one for each constraint.
To turn this constraint system into a Poly-IOP, we also need to represent the \emph{witness} by polynomials.

Let $\omega_\tau \in \mathbb{F}$ be a primitive $\tau$th root of unity, and let $H := \langle \omega_\tau \rangle$ be the subgroup of order $\tau$, which we call the \emph{trace domain}.
For simplicity, we assume that $\tau$ is a power of two; otherwise the trace is padded.
For every register $j \in [w]$, the \emph{trace polynomial} $t_j \in \mathbb{F}[T]^{<\tau}$ is the unique polynomial such that
\begin{equation*}
  t_j(\omega_\tau^i) = W_{[i,j]} \quad \text{for all} \; i \in [\tau].
\end{equation*}
In practice, it is computed with the inverse NTT (\Cref{section:ntt}).
The trace polynomials $t_0, \ldots, t_{w-1}$ represent the algebraic execution trace in terms of univariate polynomials: the $j$th column of $W$ is the restriction of $t_j$ to $H$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-3}
  We continue \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-1} and compute the trace polynomials.
  Take the trace domain $H = \langle 13 \rangle = \{1, 13, 16, 4\}$ of order $\tau = 4$, so that $\omega_\tau = 13$.
  Interpolating the columns of $W$ over $H$, we get
  \begin{equation*}
    t_0(T) = 1 + 15T + 13T^2 + 6T^3, \qquad t_1(T) = 4 + 13T + T^2 + 2T^3.
  \end{equation*}
  For instance, $t_0(\omega_\tau^2) = t_0(-1) = 1 - 15 + 13 - 6 = 10 = W_{[2,0]}$.
\end{example}

Translating the computational integrity claim to the trace polynomials, we get:
\begin{itemize}
  \item all boundary constraints are satisfied: $t_j(\omega_\tau^i) = v$ for all $(i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}$;
  \item all transition constraints are satisfied: for all $i \in [\tau - 1]$ and $u \in [\kappa]$,
  \begin{equation*}
    p_u\big(t_0(\omega_\tau^i), \ldots, t_{w-1}(\omega_\tau^i), t_0(\omega_\tau^{i+1}), \ldots, t_{w-1}(\omega_\tau^{i+1})\big) = 0.
  \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}
Here the structure of $H$ pays off: the next row is reached by multiplying the argument by $\omega_\tau$, since $\omega_\tau^{i+1} = \omega_\tau \cdot \omega_\tau^i$.
Hence, the left-hand side of the transition constraint is the univariate \emph{constraint polynomial}
\begin{equation*}
  c_u(T) := p_u\big(t_0(T), \ldots, t_{w-1}(T), t_0(\omega_\tau T), \ldots, t_{w-1}(\omega_\tau T)\big)
\end{equation*}
evaluated at $T = \omega_\tau^i$.
This just means that all $\kappa$ constraint polynomials vanish on $\{\omega_\tau^i : i \in [\tau - 1]\}$, that is, on all points of $H$ except the last one.

\subsubsection{Constraints as Divisibility}

We already know how to express ``a polynomial vanishes on a set'' as a statement about degrees: this is exactly what quotienting did in \Cref{prop:multiple-quotientings}.
By \Cref{th:root-div}, a polynomial vanishes at every point of a set $S$ if and only if it is divisible by the vanishing polynomial $\prod_{x \in S}(T - x)$.
Both kinds of AIR constraints are instances of this single principle.

\begin{definition}[Generalized AIR constraint]\label{def:gen-air}
  A \emph{generalized AIR constraint} is given by two polynomials:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \emph{numerator} determines which equation between elements of the algebraic execution trace holds, in a manner that is independent of the cycle.
    For transition constraints, the numerator is exactly the constraint polynomial $c_u$.
    For the boundary constraints of register $j$, the numerator is $t_j - \iota_j$, where $\iota_j$ interpolates the required boundary values.
    \item The \emph{denominator} is a vanishing polynomial that determines where the equation is supposed to hold, by vanishing in those points.
    For transition constraints, it vanishes on all points of the trace domain except the last.
    For boundary constraints, it vanishes only on the boundary points.
  \end{itemize}
  The constraint is satisfied if and only if the numerator is divisible by the denominator, that is, if the \emph{quotient} of the numerator by the denominator is a polynomial.
\end{definition}

Explicitly, for every register $j \in [w]$, let $\mathcal{B}_j := \{\omega_\tau^i : (i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}\}$ be its set of boundary points, let the \emph{boundary interpolant} $\iota_j$ be the polynomial of degree less than $\#\mathcal{B}_j$ such that $\iota_j(\omega_\tau^i) = v$ for all $(i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}$, and let $\nu_j(T) := \prod_{x \in \mathcal{B}_j} (T - x)$.
These are precisely the polynomials $\iota$ and $\nu$ of \Cref{prop:multiple-quotientings}, applied to the points $\mathcal{B}_j$.
The \emph{transition vanishing polynomial} is
\begin{equation*}
  \nu_{\mathcal{T}}(T) := \prod_{i \in [\tau - 1]} (T - \omega_\tau^i) = \frac{T^\tau - 1}{T - \omega_\tau^{\tau - 1}},
\end{equation*}
where the second equality follows from \Cref{lemma:vanishing-polynomial}.
The quotients are
\begin{equation*}
  q^{\mathcal{B}}_j := \frac{t_j - \iota_j}{\nu_j}, \quad j \in [w], \qquad q^{\mathcal{T}}_u := \frac{c_u}{\nu_{\mathcal{T}}}, \quad u \in [\kappa].
\end{equation*}
If $p_u$ has total degree $d_u$, then $\deg c_u \leq d_u (\tau - 1)$, so a valid trace gives $\deg q^{\mathcal{T}}_u \leq (d_u - 1)(\tau - 1)$ and $\deg q^{\mathcal{B}}_j < \tau - \#\mathcal{B}_j$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:stark-quotients}
  The trace $W$ satisfies all boundary and transition constraints if and only if all boundary quotients $q^{\mathcal{B}}_j$ and all transition quotients $q^{\mathcal{T}}_u$ are polynomials.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  A quotient is a polynomial if and only if its numerator vanishes at every root of its denominator (\Cref{th:root-div}, applied to each root in turn).
  For the boundary quotients, this means $t_j(\omega_\tau^i) = v$ for all $(i, j, v) \in \mathcal{B}$, and for the transition quotients it means $c_u(\omega_\tau^i) = 0$ for all $i \in [\tau - 1]$, which are exactly the constraints.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-4}
  We continue \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-3}.
  Substituting $X_j \mapsto t_j(T)$ and $Y_j \mapsto t_j(\omega_\tau T)$ into $p_0$ and $p_1$, we get the constraint polynomials
  \begin{align*}
    c_0(T) &= t_0(\omega_\tau T) - t_1(T) = 14 + 12T + 3T^2 + 5T^3, \\
    c_1(T) &= t_1(\omega_\tau T) - t_0(T)^2 - t_1(T)^2 \\
           &= 4 + T + 13T^2 + 6T^3 + 6T^4 + 10T^5 + 11T^6.
  \end{align*}
  Their values on $H$ are shown below: both vanish on $\omega_\tau^0, \omega_\tau^1, \omega_\tau^2$, but not on $\omega_\tau^3$, where there is no next row to compare with.
  \begin{center}

\subsubsection{AIR as a Poly-IOP}

\Cref{prop:stark-quotients} reduces the computational integrity claim to ``certain rational functions are polynomials''.
To check this with few evaluations, we combine all quotients into one with a random challenge, as in the batching of \Cref{lemma:proximity-gap-simplified},
and check the defining identity of the combination at a single random point.
For simplicity, from now on we assume that all transition verification polynomials have degree at most $2$, as in \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-2}.
Then all quotients have degree less than $\tau$.

\begin{definition}[AIR Poly-IOP]\label{def:air-poly-iop}
  The AIR $\big(\tau, w, \{p_u\}_{u \in [\kappa]}, \mathcal{B}\big)$ is public, and the prover knows a trace $W$.
  \begin{algoen}
    \item The prover sends the trace polynomials $t_0, \ldots, t_{w-1} \in \mathbb{F}[T]^{<\tau}$.
    \item The verifier samples $\gamma \leftarrowS \mathbb{F}$, and the prover sends the \emph{composition polynomial}
    \begin{equation*}
      \psi := \sum_{u \in [\kappa]} \gamma^u \cdot q^{\mathcal{T}}_u + \sum_{j \in [w]} \gamma^{\kappa + j} \cdot q^{\mathcal{B}}_j \in \mathbb{F}[T]^{<\tau}.
    \end{equation*}
    \item The verifier samples $r_0 \leftarrowS \mathbb{F}$, queries $t_j(r_0)$ and $t_j(\omega_\tau r_0)$ for all $j \in [w]$, and $\psi(r_0)$.
    Writing $\mathbf{t}(r_0) := (t_0(r_0), \ldots, t_{w-1}(r_0))$, it checks that
    \begin{equation*}
      \psi(r_0) = \sum_{u \in [\kappa]} \gamma^u \cdot \frac{p_u\big(\mathbf{t}(r_0), \mathbf{t}(\omega_\tau r_0)\big)}{\nu_{\mathcal{T}}(r_0)} + \sum_{j \in [w]} \gamma^{\kappa + j} \cdot \frac{t_j(r_0) - \iota_j(r_0)}{\nu_j(r_0)}.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{algoen}
\end{definition}

The right-hand side is computed by the verifier itself from the queried values and the public polynomials $p_u$, $\iota_j$, $\nu_j$ and $\nu_{\mathcal{T}}$, just as it computed the quotients $\mathsf{q}_j$ cell by cell in \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}.
If the trace is valid, then by \Cref{prop:stark-quotients} the right-hand side is the evaluation of the polynomial $\psi$ at $r_0$, so the check passes.
If some constraint fails, then some quotient is not a polynomial, and with high probability over $\gamma$ neither is their combination; so no polynomial $\psi$ can agree with it at a random point $r_0$, except with probability about $2\tau/\#\mathbb{F}$ by \Cref{lemma:one-sz}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-5}
  We continue \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-4} with $\gamma = 2$.
  The composition polynomial is
  \begin{equation*}
    \psi = q^{\mathcal{T}}_0 + 2 q^{\mathcal{T}}_1 + 4 q^{\mathcal{B}}_0 + 8 q^{\mathcal{B}}_1 = 4 + 12T + 6T^2 + 5T^3.
  \end{equation*}
  Suppose the verifier samples $r_0 = 2$, so that $\omega_\tau r_0 = 9$.
  It learns $t_0(2) = 12$, $t_0(9) = 4$, $t_1(2) = 16$, $t_1(9) = 11$ and $\psi(2) = 7$.
  It computes $p_0 = 4 - 16 = 5$, $p_1 = 11 - 12^2 - 16^2 = 2$ and $\nu_{\mathcal{T}}(2) = 1$, and checks
  \begin{equation*}
    5 + 2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot \frac{12 - 1}{2 - 1} + 8 \cdot \frac{16 - 13}{2 - 4} = 7 = \psi(2).
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}

\subsubsection{Compiling the AIR Poly-IOP}

The AIR Poly-IOP sends $w + 1$ polynomials of degree less than $\tau$ and queries them at the two points $r_0$ and $\omega_\tau r_0$.
This is exactly the input of the compilation of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}, with $m = w + 1$ polynomials and $t = 1$ additional point:
\begin{algoen}
  \item \textbf{Commit.} The prover sends the restrictions $\overline{t_0}, \ldots, \overline{t_{w-1}}$ and $\overline{\psi}$ to an evaluation domain $\Omega$ of size $n = \tau / \rho$.
  \item \textbf{Bind and query.} The point $r_0$ is already a random point, so it doubles as the binding point of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-second-attempt}; we only need $r_0 \notin \Omega$. The prover sends the claimed values at $r_0$ and $\omega_\tau r_0$.
  \item \textbf{Quotient and batch.} Both parties define the virtual quotients $\mathsf{q}_j := (g_j - \iota'_j)/\nu'$ with $\nu' := (T - r_0)(T - \omega_\tau r_0)$, where $g_j$ runs over the committed oracles and $\iota'_j$ interpolates the claimed values, and batch them into $h := \sum_j \alpha^j \mathsf{q}_j$.
  \item \textbf{Test.} The parties run FRI (\Cref{def:fri}) on $h$ against $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}, \Omega, \tau - 2]$.
\end{algoen}
The resulting protocol, after the BCS transformation, is a STARK.
It is essentially the DEEP-ALI protocol used by deployed STARKs~\cite{ITCS:BGKS20}. % TODO: bib key

There is one subtlety: the domain $\Omega$.
The verifier divides by $\nu_{\mathcal{T}}$ and $\nu_j$, which vanish on $H$, and by $\nu'$, which vanishes at $r_0$ and $\omega_\tau r_0$; and FRI needs $-x \in \Omega$ for every $x \in \Omega$.
The standard choice is a coset of a larger subgroup.

\begin{remark}\label{remark:stark-coset}
  Let $\omega$ be a primitive $n$th root of unity with $\omega^{n/\tau} = \omega_\tau$, so that $H \subseteq \langle \omega \rangle$, and take $\Omega := c \cdot \langle \omega \rangle$ for some $c \notin \langle \omega \rangle$.
  Then $\Omega \cap H = \emptyset$.
  FRI works on a coset in exactly the same way: $-x \in \Omega$ for every $x \in \Omega$, and squaring maps $\Omega$ onto the coset $c^2 \cdot \langle \omega^2 \rangle$ of half the size.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-6}
  We continue \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-5} with $\rho = 1/2$, so $n = \tau / \rho = 8$.
  Let $\omega = 9$, which has order $8$ in $\mathbb{F}_{17}^\times$, so that $\omega^2 = 13 = \omega_\tau$ and
  \begin{equation*}
    \langle \omega \rangle = \{1, 9, 13, 15, 16, 8, 4, 2\} \supseteq H.
  \end{equation*}
  The element $c = 3$ does not belong to $\langle \omega \rangle$, so we take
  \begin{equation*}
    \Omega = 3 \cdot \langle \omega \rangle = \{3, 10, 5, 11, 14, 7, 12, 6\},
  \end{equation*}
  where the $i$th element is $3 \cdot \omega^i$.
  Indeed, $\Omega \cap H = \emptyset$, and the point $r_0 = 2$ from \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-5} and $\omega_\tau r_0 = 9$ lie outside $\Omega$ as well.
  The prover commits to the following three codewords:
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[
        x=0.8cm, y=0.6cm,

        dom/.style={cell, fill=oc-gray-1},

    ]
        \foreach \x/\a/\b/\c [count=\i from 0] in {3/2/4/8, 10/5/7/12, 5/12/4/6, 11/1/6/3, 14/13/5/6, 7/13/14/6, 12/11/3/13, 6/2/6/12} {
            \node[dom] at (\i, 0) {\x};
            \node[cell, fill=oc-blue-1] at (\i, -1) {\a};
            \node[cell, fill=oc-blue-1] at (\i, -2) {\b};
            \node[cell, fill=oc-blue-1] at (\i, -3) {\c};

  Besides the values at $r_0 = 2$ from \Cref{ex:stark-fibsq-5}, the prover sends $t_0(9) = 4$, $t_1(9) = 11$ and $\psi(9) = 10$.
  With $\nu' = (T - 2)(T - 9)$, the three quotients are $\mathsf{q}_0 = 11 + 6T$, $\mathsf{q}_1 = 6 + 2T$ and $\mathsf{q}_2 = 10 + 5T$, and the batching challenge $\alpha = 3$ gives
  \begin{equation*}
    h = \mathsf{q}_0 + 3 \mathsf{q}_1 + 9 \mathsf{q}_2 = 6T,
  \end{equation*}
  a codeword of $\mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}_{17}, \Omega, 2]$.
  FRI needs a single fold: with $\beta_0 = 5$, the fold of $\overline{h}$ is the constant $13$ on $\Omega^2 = \{9, 15, 8, 2\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:stark-fibsq-7}

  Since $h \in \mathsf{RS}[\mathbb{F}_{17}, \Omega, 2]$, FRI has a single round: the input layer is the virtual function $h$, and after $\beta_0 = 5$ the prover sends the constant $c = 13$.
  Suppose the verifier samples $x_0 = 3$, so it needs $h(3)$ and $h(-3) = h(14)$.
  It opens the committed codewords at $3$ and $14$:

  In the same way, $\mathsf{q}_1(3) = 12$ and $\mathsf{q}_2(3) = 8$, hence
  \begin{equation*}
    h(3) = 12 + 3 \cdot 12 + 9 \cdot 8 = 1.

If the trace is correct, the honest prover is always accepted.
If it is not, then by \Cref{prop:stark-quotients} at least one quotient is not a polynomial, so the identity of $\psi$ fails at a random $r_0$; and since the values at $r_0$ are bound to the committed codewords by quotienting and FRI, the prover cannot answer with values of a different polynomial.

\begin{remark}
  An alternative, used in~\cite{szepieniec2021anatomy}, avoids the out-of-domain point: the prover commits to the boundary quotients instead of the trace, and FRI is run directly on a random combination of all quotients, in which every quotient $q$ with degree bound $d_q$ is also multiplied by $T^{\,k - 1 - d_q}$.
  These factors are the \emph{degree correction} of \Cref{fig:poly-iop-third-attempt}: without them, a quotient whose degree exceeds its own bound but not $k$ would go unnoticed.
  Without an out-of-domain point, however, the known soundness bounds are weaker, which is why deployed STARKs use the protocol above.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
  The protocol described in this section is not zero-knowledge: the opened values of the trace polynomials and of $\psi$ reveal information about the trace.
  The original construction~\cite{EPRINT:BBHR18} does provide zero-knowledge, which is achieved by randomizing the trace polynomials outside of $H$ and masking the input of FRI with a random polynomial; see~\cite{EPRINT:HabKin24} for a detailed treatment.
  However, many practical applications of STARKs, such as validity rollups, only need succinct verification of computational integrity, so they do not use the zero-knowledge property at all.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
  As with FRI, the whole protocol is made non-interactive using the Fiat--Shamir transformation (\Cref{subsection:fiat-shamir}).
\end{remark}
